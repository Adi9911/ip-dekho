Public IP vs Private IP: The Complete Guide

Every device that connects to the internet needs an address so that data knows where to go. That address is called an IP address (Internet Protocol address). But not all IP addresses are the same. There are two main types you should understand: your public IP address and your private (local) IP address. Knowing the difference helps you troubleshoot network problems, set up devices like CCTV cameras and printers, secure your connection, and understand your online privacy.

What is a Public IP Address?

A public IP address is the address assigned to your network by your Internet Service Provider (ISP). It is the address the entire internet sees when you visit a website, send an email, or stream a video. Think of it as the street address of your home — mail carriers (websites and servers) use it to deliver data to you. Because it is visible to the outside world, your public IP can reveal your approximate location (city, region, country) and the name of your ISP. This is exactly the information shown at the top of this page. Most home connections use a dynamic public IP that changes from time to time, while businesses often pay for a static public IP that never changes — useful for hosting servers, remote access, and security systems.

What is a Private (Local) IP Address?

A private IP address is used inside your own home or office network. Your Wi-Fi router hands out private addresses to every device connected to it — your phone, laptop, smart TV, CCTV cameras, and printers. These addresses usually start with 192.168.x.x, 10.x.x.x, or 172.16.x.x. They are not visible to the wider internet; they only work within your local network. This is why two different homes can both use 192.168.1.1 without any conflict. Your router uses a process called NAT (Network Address Translation) to let many private devices share a single public IP.

Public IP vs Private IP: Key Differences

The simplest way to remember the difference: your public IP is global and unique across the whole internet, while your private IP is local and reusable inside your network. Public IPs are assigned by your ISP and can be traced to a general location; private IPs are assigned by your own router and stay hidden behind it. Public IPs are used for communication between networks; private IPs are used for communication between devices in the same network. When you troubleshoot "no internet" issues, the public IP tells you whether your ISP link is up, while the private IP tells you whether your device is properly connected to the router.

How to Check Your IP Address

Checking your public IP is easy — this page detects it automatically using trusted lookup services and displays it in big, bold text at the top, along with your ISP, city, region, country, and timezone. To find your private IP manually, on Windows you can open Command Prompt and type ipconfig, on macOS or Linux type ifconfig or ip addr in the Terminal, and on a phone you can look under Wi-Fi settings for the connected network. Our tool also attempts to detect your local IP directly in the browser using WebRTC, so you can see both addresses in one place without typing any commands.

Why Companies Need to Know Their IP Address

For businesses, IP addresses are more than a technical detail — they are the backbone of secure operations. Companies use static public IPs to host websites and email servers, to allow employees to connect remotely through a VPN, and to whitelist trusted connections in firewalls. IT teams rely on private IP schemes to organize hundreds of devices, assign fixed addresses to servers and network printers, and keep CCTV and access-control systems reliable. A well-planned IP structure reduces downtime, improves security, and makes it far easier to scale as the organization grows. This is where professional support matters: correctly configuring routers, subnets, port forwarding, and firewalls prevents the outages and security gaps that cost businesses time and money.

Protecting Your IP and Staying Private

Because your public IP can hint at your location, some users prefer to hide it. A VPN (Virtual Private Network) routes your traffic through another server, so websites see the VPN's IP instead of yours. Proxy servers and the Tor network offer similar privacy at different trade-offs in speed and convenience. For everyday users, simply keeping your router firmware updated, using a strong Wi-Fi password, and enabling your firewall goes a long way. Remember that your IP address alone does not reveal your name, phone number, or exact home — but combined with other data it can narrow things down, which is why privacy-conscious browsing is worth practicing.

Understanding Your Internet Speed

Alongside your IP details, this page includes a built-in speed test that measures four important numbers. Download speed is how fast data reaches you — it affects streaming, browsing, and downloads. Upload speed is how fast you can send data — important for video calls, backups, and uploading CCTV footage to the cloud. Ping (latency) is the delay before data starts moving, measured in milliseconds; lower is better, especially for gaming and video conferencing. Jitter measures how consistent your ping is — high jitter causes choppy calls and lag. Running this test regularly helps you confirm you are getting the speeds your ISP promised, and gives you evidence if you need to raise a complaint or upgrade your plan.

Whether you are a home user curious about your connection or a business planning a reliable network, understanding public and private IP addresses — and keeping an eye on your speed — puts you in control.
